The city below was a smear of gold and white, a schematic of someone else’s life. From the forty-seventh floor of the Iris Lounge, the noise of the streets was a low, systemic hum, the sound of a machine working as intended. Adam ran a thumb over the cold sweat of his glass. The ice inside had melted an hour ago.

“To Adam,” Karim said, his voice thick from the whiskey. He raised his glass, the liquid catching the light. “The God-Brother. He pulls you out of the fire and doesn’t even ask if you’re burned.”

Bassam and the others echoed the toast, a ragged chorus of gratitude. They were good men, talented men, artists and engineers whose plans were always grander than their credit lines. Adam’s friends. His assets.

Karim slumped back into the plush velvet of the banquette, his face slack with relief. “Five thousand. He was going to send men to my father’s shop, Adam. He said he’d break the windows.”

“The windows are insured,” Adam said. He didn’t look at Karim, but at the city. “Your father’s hands are not. I sent the transfer. It’s done.”

A server in a starched black jacket materialized at his elbow. “Another round of the Macallan, Mr. Mansour?”

“For them,” Adam said, gesturing to the table. “And bring the bill.”

Under the cool marble of the table, his left hand held his phone. His thumb was slick on the glass screen. A single chart glowed in the darkness, a jagged line of green and red candlesticks. Bitcoin was bleeding out, dragging the altcoins down with it, a cascade of liquidations pulling the market into a vortex. He was short. A five-hundred-thousand-dollar position, leveraged fifty times over. A bet against the world’s blind optimism, collateralized with everything he had and several things he didn’t. His liquidation price was a whisper away. One sharp spike, one fat finger on an exchange in another hemisphere, and it would all evaporate. His family’s house, Layla’s tuition, the silent promissory note from a man who did not forgive delays—all of it would turn to smoke.

A nervous twitch started in his left eyelid. He felt it flutter, a trapped bird behind the skin.

“You’re a king, Adam, you know that?” Karim’s words were a warm balm. “A prince. What you do for us…”

“I put out fires,” Adam said, his eyes still on the screen. The price was stalling, hovering just above his line of death. A wall of buy orders was holding it up. A flimsy dam against a flood. “It’s what a brother does.”

He wasn’t watching the price. He was watching the order book. The flow. The depth of the bids and asks. It was a language he understood better than the one they were speaking at the table. It was the pure, honest grammar of fear and greed. He saw the buy wall begin to thin, eaten away by sellers desperate to get out. The dam was breaking.

Come on.

His thumb hovered over the red ‘Market Close’ button. He could exit now, with a small profit. A safe, sensible move. The kind of move his father would make, securing a small dignity while the opportunity for a kingdom slipped away.

The twitch in his eye beat faster. He held.

Bassam, quiet until now, leaned forward. He had the worried eyes of a man who still believed in things like souls. “Is everything alright, Adam? You’ve been staring at your lap all night.”

Adam looked up, forcing a smile that felt like it might crack his face. “Just admiring the view.”

And then it happened. On the screen, a waterfall of red. The buy wall vanished, consumed in an instant. The price plunged, a stone falling from a tower. His PnL—Profit and Loss—flashed green, the numbers climbing so fast they were a blur. Twenty thousand. Fifty. One hundred. He let it run, his heart hammering against his ribs, a wild drumbeat of victory. He felt the spike of adrenaline, clean and sharp, a current that made the world stand still. This was it. The moment of creation. The feeling of bending the universe to his will with nothing but nerve and a clean line of credit.

He let it fall for another ten seconds, a freefall into profit, before his thumb finally stabbed the screen. Position closed.

A breath he didn’t know he was holding left his lungs in a silent rush. The twitch in his eyelid stopped. The city lights outside seemed to sharpen, to burn brighter. He was the master of this machine. He was its ghost.

The server returned, placing a small leather billfold on the table. Adam opened it. Three thousand, two hundred and twenty dollars. He pulled his wallet out, the worn leather soft in his hand, and slid out a black metal card. He dropped it into the folder without looking at the numbers again.

“It’s handled,” he said. He finally met Karim’s eyes, and saw the reverence there, the worship. It was better than the money. It was the entire point of the money.

The ride-share smelled of synthetic pine and old sweat. As the car left the gleaming towers of the financial district, the streets grew narrower, the lights dimmer. The smooth asphalt gave way to cracked pavement. Here, the buildings leaned on each other for support, their limestone balconies crumbling like stale bread. Adam rested his head against the cool glass of the window, the vibration of the engine a dull drone. The exhilaration of the trade had faded, leaving behind the familiar, metallic tang of exhaustion. He could still feel the phantom glow of the screen on his retinas. A profit of one hundred and seventy-two thousand dollars. Enough to keep the walls from closing in for another month. Enough to buy more time.

The car stopped at the end of his street. He paid the driver in the app, the number just another abstraction, and stepped out into the damp night air. It smelled of boiled chicory from Mrs. Hachem’s kitchen and the sharp scent of floor disinfectant used to fight a losing war against the building’s inherent decay.

He pushed open the heavy iron-and-glass door. It groaned on its hinges. The lobby was dark, save for a single, fly-specked bulb that cast a sickly yellow light. His footsteps echoed in the stairwell as he began the climb to the fourth floor. The air was thick with the history of every meal ever cooked here, every argument whispered behind thin walls.

On the landing between the third and fourth floors, he stopped. A sound. A dry, rattling cough that seemed to tear at the silence. It was followed by the soft scrape of a shoe on concrete, the sound of a heavy weight being shifted.

Adam rounded the corner.

His father stood in the darkness of the hallway, leaning against the wall. Yousef Mansour, seventy-six years old, a man who had once drafted the blueprints for half the banks in the new city, was breathing in shallow, ragged gasps. One hand was braced against the peeling paint of the wall. In the other, he clutched a thin plastic bag. Through the transparent plastic, Adam could see a bundle of cheap, flat bread. His father’s coat, a woolen relic from a colder country and a younger life, was pulled tight around his thin shoulders.

For a moment, neither of them moved. Adam’s heart, which had soared with the numbers on the screen, now felt like a stone in his chest. The three-thousand-dollar bar tab was a vulgarity that burned behind his eyes.

“Baba?” Adam’s voice was too loud in the quiet stairwell.

Yousef startled, his head snapping up. His spectacles were smudged, catching the dim light. He straightened his back with a pained effort, a flash of the old pride warring with the exhaustion on his face.

“Adam. You are home late.” His voice was a dry rasp.

“I was with friends.” Adam closed the distance between them, his expensive sneakers silent on the worn tiles. He reached for the bag of bread. “Let me.”

His father’s hand tightened on the plastic for a second, a reflexive refusal to be a burden. Then the fight went out of him. His fingers loosened, and Adam took the bag. It weighed almost nothing.

“The baker on the corner, he gives me the day-old for half the price,” Yousef said, as if it were a shrewd business decision. He started moving again, his hand trailing along the wall for balance. Each step was a deliberate, fragile act.

Adam followed him in silence, the weight of the bread in his hand a hundred times heavier than the half-million-dollar position he had managed an hour ago. He unlocked the apartment door, the familiar clicks of the three separate locks a small, percussive ritual. He pushed it open into the darkness of their home.

The apartment smelled of his mother’s rosewater and the faint, sulfurous hiss of the pilot light on the ancient gas water heater. It smelled of thirty years of preserved stillness. A light clicked on in the kitchen. His mother stood there, a silhouette in her worn housecoat, her hands already moving to the stove. She always waited up.

“Adam, habibi, you’re here.” She didn’t look at him, her attention on a small pot. “You look like a ghost. Sit. I’ll heat the soup.”

Yousef shuffled past them both, toward his small bedroom at the end of the hall, the sound of his woolen coat brushing against the wall a dry, weary whisper. He didn’t say goodnight.

Adam sat at the small kitchen table. Every object in this room was a monument to a life of careful preservation. The crocheted doilies on the back of the chairs. The stacks of his father’s old legal files in cardboard boxes, tied with string. This was the gravity he fought against every day. The immense, crushing weight of the past.

His mother placed a bowl of lentil soup in front of him. Steam rose from it, carrying the scent of cumin and lemon. It was the food of his childhood. The food of lean years. She stood behind his chair, her hand resting on his head. Her fingers were calloused but gentle. He felt her lips press against his hair, and heard her whisper a prayer, the words a soft, familiar current against his scalp. A prayer for protection. For guidance. For a son who moved in a world of fire she could not understand.

He picked up the spoon. The soup was hot, scalding his tongue. He ate, each spoonful a penance. His mother’s hand on his shoulder felt less like a comfort and more like a yoke. He was their pillar. Their savior. If he fell, the whole house, this whole history, would collapse into the street. The thought was not a comfort. It was a cage.

Later, in his own room, the door shut against the sleeping sounds of the apartment, he pulled out his phone. The air was thick, heavy with the scent of his mother’s soup and his father’s quiet defeat. He opened the trading app. His balance glowed in the dark. A large number. A powerful number.

It meant nothing here.

He swiped to his calendar. A reminder was flagged for next week. *Layla—Tuition Due*. Another fire, already burning on the horizon. He stared at the number on the screen, the profit from the night’s victory. It was not a cushion. It was just ammunition for the next battle. He rubbed his thumb over his index finger, counting invisible bills in the suffocating dark.
