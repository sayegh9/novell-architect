The noise was a fist of light punching through his skull. Orange bursts from the smithy next door, the percussive clang of a sonic hammer shaping steel. A saw-toothed line of angry red, the screech of a new apprentice mishandling a lathe two benches down. The air was thick with the grit of sound, a storm of jagged color that left a taste like rust on his tongue.

Cassian hunched over the workbench, trying to shrink the world to the small bronze bell in his left hand. He tapped its tines with a felt-headed mallet. The sound that rose was a warm, liquid bronze, the color of the metal itself. But around its edges shimmered a thin, acidic halo of yellow-green. Dissonance. A fraction of a cent too high, a sharpness that felt like a needle sliding in just behind his eye.

His jaw tightened. He drew a fine-toothed file across the inner lip, a delicate scrape of steel on bronze. The motion produced a low, grating hiss like tearing cloth. He was trying to shave away the yellow-green, to file down the impurity until only the pure bronze remained. He filed, tapped, and saw the same sickly halo persist. A little thinner, maybe. But still there. An imperfection. His failure.

Heavy footsteps on the stone floor behind him. Each one a solid, gray thud that didn't so much cut through the workshop’s noise as flatten it. Cassian did not need to turn. The chaotic colors seemed to dim in the presence of this new, cold frequency.

His father stood over the bench, a shadow falling across the bell. Eddan reached out a broad, calloused hand and took it from its cradle. Cassian’s own hands felt cold and empty.

Eddan produced his own mallet. He hummed a single note, a perfect C-sharp that Cassian saw as a column of unwavering granite-gray light. A sound with weight. The sound of law. He tapped the bell.

The bronze tone rose to meet the gray one. They clashed.

The dissonance was a physical blow. A grinding, shimmering interference pattern flared between the two columns of light, a painful, strobing pulse that made Cassian’s teeth ache. The thin yellow-green halo around his bell’s note scraped against his father’s pure tone like a rusted nail on slate. The needle behind his eye twisted. He winced.

Eddan set the bell down on the bench with a dull click. A final judgment.

“It is sharp,” Eddan said. His voice was flat, colder than anger. “The overtone is impure.”

“I was close,” Cassian managed, the words tasting of metal dust.

“Close is a word for masons laying stone,” his father said. “We are Bellwrights. We work in purity. A bell that is ‘close’ is noise.” He picked up the file from the bench. “Precision is not a goal, Cassian. It is the only state of being.”

As he drew his left hand back, Cassian saw it. A tremor. A fine, constant shudder in his father’s fingers, a desperate, losing battle for stillness. The Tremor, the signature decay of their Guild.

Eddan followed his son’s gaze. He saw the flicker of pity.

The shame that flashed from him was a dark, ugly red, like blood in dirty water. He snatched the hand back, shoving it deep into the pocket of his leather apron. The shame curdled instantly to a shield of cold fury.

“This floor is a disgrace,” Eddan snapped, his eyes sweeping the workshop. He was looking at anything but Cassian. “If you cannot perform the work of a journeyman, you will perform the work of a boy. Clean it.”

The order was a blade. Cassian gave a short, stiff nod.

His father turned and walked away, his heavy, gray footsteps receding into the workshop’s riot of color.

The rhythmic scrape and push of the broom was a low, scratching sound, the color of dried mud. Cassian swept up glittering piles of bronze filings and curls of wood shavings, working his way methodically down the length of the room. He pushed the broom into a dark corner beneath a rack of cooling bells, a space cluttered with forgotten tools and splintered crates. One of them bore Perin’s initials, burned into the lid.

He knelt, running a thumb over the letters. His brother’s things, meant to be sent for but never were. He lifted the lid. Inside, nestled in wood shavings, was a simple iron tuning fork—Perin’s first. Cassian remembered Perin striking it, his face alight. The sound had been perfect, of course. Everything Perin did was perfect. A clear, golden note that Cassian saw as a thread of pure sunlight. *That’s the Bind, Cass,* Perin had said, his voice bright with the same golden light. *The sound that builds the world.*

The Corda contract had taken that voice. The memory was a fresh pang in his ribs, a hollow space where the light had been. He closed the crate.

He worked his way to the back wall, a place of deep shadows where the air was cooler, stiller. Here, set into the massive stone of the foundation, was a door made of thick, dark oak, banded with black iron. There was no handle, no latch, only a complex lock of interlocking brass rings. He had been forbidden to touch it for his entire life.

He had swept this floor a thousand times, but today he stopped. He looked at the door, at the absolute stillness of the air around it. The chaotic colors of the workshop seemed to bend away from it, leaving a small pocket of quiet darkness. It did not absorb sound; it negated it. A void.

He swept the last of the dust away from its heavy threshold. He leaned on the broom, the rough wood a solid presence in his hands. He looked at the intricate brass lock, a puzzle he was not allowed to solve. Behind it was the only true silence in the House of Bells. He had no idea what it was keeping in, or what it was keeping out.
